<p>But Liu had no reason to rush. With his arms crossed, he sank deep into the seat and gazed out the car window at the excited, flushed faces of the people passing by. New York's average winter temperature was higher than Seoul's, but the cold here had a strange way of making one feel more chilled than the actual temperature warranted.</p>

<p>A light drizzle was falling on the streets. The driver had the radio on at low volume, and a reporter's voice was excitedly announcing that two million people had gathered for the Ball Drop.</p>

<p>Imagining Times Square packed with two million people gave him the start of a tension headache. But it wasn't the crowd that caused it. He could function when focused on work, but any small lapse in concentration brought on intense stress. His only self-prescribed remedy for the headaches and insomnia that had plagued him for months was alcohol and cigarettes.</p>

<p>Liu rolled down the window halfway and lit a cigarette. The street noise grew a little closer. A group of five or six people, looking to be in their early to mid-twenties, passed by loudly blowing vuvuzelas. He stubbed out the cigarette after just a few puffs and rolled the window back up.</p>

<p>He wouldn't have felt this low if he hadn't received that call from the hotel this afternoon.</p>

<p>The hotel had called to confirm with a guest who had reserved a room — one costing well over ten thousand dollars a night, booked at an even higher rate than usual — and then failed to show up. Liu had forgotten the reservation entirely until the call came through. It was an unnecessary courtesy.</p>

<p>It was a room he had booked assuming he would come to New York with Ihyeon, so he could watch the Ball Drop in comfort.</p>

<p>Securing a room with a direct view of the New Year's Eve Ball had required calling in favors — a whole operation in itself. Now it was just unnecessary baggage. A gift with no one left to receive it.</p>

<p>The hotel employee had hesitantly explained that no refund was possible even without checking in. After Liu's indifferent reply, the employee offered a cheerful New Year's greeting and disappeared from the line. The employee might have felt relieved. Liu found it difficult to concentrate on anything afterward.</p>

<p>He had left Seoul as if fleeing from memories of Ihyeon, yet even here — in a place he had never been with him — he could not escape him.</p>

<p>As the headache tightened its grip, Liu finally began drinking the whiskey he had brought in the car. Only after slowly finishing two glasses could he manage to reach his apartment. He apologized to the H&W driver for not having a separate gift prepared, handed over a tip as a substitute, and offered a brief New Year's greeting before stepping out. One of the doormen recognized his car, quickly unfurled an umbrella, and came hurrying toward him.</p>

<p>It was only after getting into the elevator and being alone that he felt he could breathe a little easier. Inside the reserved, upscale condominium on the Upper East Side, the festive atmosphere of the streets felt distant — a celebration from another world. That was a relief.</p>

<p>As soon as he entered the foyer, he tossed his coat onto a stool in front of the kitchen counter and opened the refrigerator to pull out a beer.</p>
